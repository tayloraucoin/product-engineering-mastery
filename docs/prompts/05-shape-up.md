# Prompt 05 — Shape Up, read for a seed-stage product engineer in 2026

**Model:** Sonnet
**Inject:** Compass (Product Strategist) — appetite, betting, and shaping are product decisions; Compass owns the "whether" and hands sequencing to delivery.
**Attach:** `00-shared-context`, the Compass role prompt (§7 filled for DealReady if you have it; otherwise let Compass label its assumptions), textbook Part 1.3 and Part 6.
**Expected output:** an investigation report on Shape Up as written, then a ruling on what I adopt, what I adapt for AI-speed building, and what I leave.

---

Compass — you're on this one. Read your role prompt and the shared context first.

**The decision this serves.** I'm setting the operating rhythm for a seed-stage team where I'm Head of Product and one agent-equipped engineer can ship what used to take a squad a cycle. Shape Up's core moves — appetite instead of estimates, shaping before betting, fixed time and variable scope, the betting table, cool-down — are the ones the textbook flagged as *more* important now that build time compresses. I want the method understood from the source, then judged against our reality, so I can write our cycle rules without cargo-culting Basecamp.

**The ask.**

1. Shape Up as Ryan Singer wrote it, from the free book itself (basecamp.com/shapeup) — not from summaries. The concepts in order: appetite, shaping (breadboarding, fat-marker sketches, rabbit holes, no-gos), the pitch, the betting table, six-week cycles and two-week cool-downs, hill charts, scope hammering, circuit breakers, "done means deployed." Quote the definitions. Note what the book says about team size and stage, because it was written for a company that was not seed-stage.
2. The critiques and adaptations that have real substance: what teams report breaking when they adopt it at small scale, what Singer himself has revised since (his later writing and talks), and how continuous-discovery practitioners (Torres) reconcile it with weekly customer contact. Secondary sources named.
3. The AI-speed question, argued both ways: if a six-week bet can now be built in six days, does appetite get smaller, do cycles get shorter, or does the constraint move from build time to discovery and verification time? Where does Recipe A's verify step sit inside a cycle? What does a "pitch" look like when the prototype can be built before the betting table meets?
4. The ruling for us: cycle length, appetite sizes, what a pitch must contain (map it to my `brief.md` fields — job, user, metric, constraints, non-goals, states), who sits at the betting table when the table is two people, what cool-down is for when there's no backlog to groom, and the circuit breaker rule. Name what you're rejecting from the book and why.
5. The displaced item: what current habit or ritual this replaces, so the change is a subtraction and not an addition.

**Evidence rules.** The book is primary. Singer's own later posts are primary for his revisions. Everything else is secondary and named. Don't invent what the book says about AI; it predates it.

**Output shape.** Verdict; the method as written (structured, with quotes and page or chapter references); the critiques; the AI-speed argument; the ruling as a one-page cycle charter I can paste into the team handbook; the displaced item; sources.

**Done means** I have the cycle charter and can run the first betting table next week.

**Not wanted:** a book summary with no ruling, or a ruling that ignores that our engineer ships with agents and our discovery cadence is weekly.
