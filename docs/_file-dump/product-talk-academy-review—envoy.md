## 1. Verdict

**The program is alive, but it is not the program the textbook describes, and the flagship is no longer the right buy for you.**

- **Alive:** yes. The course pages on producttalk.org were republished on 27 Sep 2026, Torres published on 2 and 16 Sep 2026, the book club shipped its Chapter 10 guide on 21 Sep 2026, and a quarterly live session ran 17 Sep 2026. `[verified]`
- **The "dated website" is one surface, not the business.** The stale look lives on `learn.producttalk.org` (the enrolment and course host), whose meta description still says "15,000+ … six highly-rated courses" while its own body copy says 18,000+ students — and five of those six courses were closed to new enrolment in January. The marketing site was rebuilt on Ghost and is current. `[verified]` The red flag is real, but it points at a catalogue in transition, not a dead program. `[judgment]`
- **Thinned, plainly:** the five Deep Dive cohort courses were sunset in January 2026; the plan to replace them with 15 on-demand courses has shipped 3 of 15 as of 27 Sep 2026 (the same three that existed on 14 Jan); and Torres taught her last Fundamentals cohort "in its current format" in June–July 2026 — cohorts are now taught by the instructor team. Her own output has moved decisively toward building AI products and Claude Code workflows. `[verified]`
- **Recommendation:** do **not** enrol in Product Discovery Fundamentals ($1,795). It is an introductory course practised on a case study, not your product, and you have already read the book. **Do** buy the on-demand **Story-Based Customer Interviews** course ($259, three months) when your first interviews are booked, for one reason: the Interview Coach gives graded feedback on your own practice interviews, which is the one thing the book cannot. Buy **Customer Recruiting for Continuous Discovery** ($259) only if you miss the DealReady interview target two weeks running. `[judgment]`

The disconfirming condition that would change this: a Fundamentals cohort taught by Torres in a new AI-coached format, at a Pacific-friendly time, before your cycle charter is set. None was found. `[not found]`

## 3. What the program teaches beyond the book

### Product Discovery Fundamentals — $1,795, six weeks, instructor team

Syllabus as published: Defining Clear Outcomes · Continuous Interviewing · Opportunity Mapping · Effective Ideation · Identifying Hidden Assumptions · Assumption Testing — one habit per week, applied live to a shared case study in Zoom breakouts and a shared Miro board. `[verified]`

What it adds over the book, from its own pages: live small-group practice, instructor facilitation, peers, and a completion certificate. Between-session application to your own work is encouraged but explicitly optional ("it won't impede your progress"). Structured data on an earlier cohort page puts the workload at 12–18 hours. `[verified]`

What it does **not** publicly offer: individual feedback on your own interviews, your own tree, or your own assumption tests. `[not found]` Per-session lesson content beyond the six headings: `[NOT IN SOURCE]` (paid).

`[judgment]` For a reader of the book running a live product, this is the book performed in a room. Its value is the rehearsal and the cohort, and the rehearsal is on someone else's case.

### Story-Based Customer Interviews — $259, three months, on-demand

Seven modules, 28 lessons, about 11 hours of video, of which about 5.4 hours is the **Interview Vault**: 15 real 20–30 minute interviews Torres conducted (5 B2C with AnyList customers, 10 B2B with Lovable customers). Module 01 opens with an hour on separating research questions from interview questions. The **Interview Coach** takes a recorded practice interview and returns question-by-question feedback. The completion certificate requires submitting one of your own interviews for instructor verification. Monthly office hours and Slack are included for the term. `[verified]`

`[judgment]` This is the part the book structurally cannot give: deliberate practice with feedback on *your* questions, plus ten B2B interviews to model from.

### Customer Recruiting for Continuous Discovery — $259, three months, on-demand

Six modules, 49 lessons, 5.5 hours. Four strategies (in-product, customer-facing-team triggers, customer advisory board / design partners, email outreach) plus a lesson on recruiting before you have a product; 12 case studies, 13 critiques of real recruiting asks; tool walkthroughs (Typeform, Qualaroo, Ethn.io, Calendly, Reclaim, Cal.com, User Interviews); instructor review of your recruiting plan. `[verified]`

`[judgment]` Of the three, this is the most directly load-bearing for DealReady, where recruiting senior investors weekly is the hard part — but only once recruiting is actually the bottleneck.

### CDH Membership — $359 for 12 months

A one-time payment for community, calls, office hours, and extended access to any course previously purchased. `[verified]` Not worth buying standalone. `[judgment]`

## 4. Has the method absorbed AI?

**Torres's public positions (her own words, paraphrased unless quoted):**

- **Synthesis:** good synthesis is two steps — per interview first, then across interviews — and most AI tools skip the first step. She reports that Claude caught opportunities she missed and she caught things it missed, and cites research that expert-plus-AI beats either alone. (18 Feb 2026) `[verified]`
- **Lazy synthesis is the main anti-pattern:** her practice is to synthesize herself first, then have AI do it, then compare. (airfocus interview, ~Apr 2026) `[secondary]`
- **Synthetic users:** not ready for prime time. (All Things Product show notes, Jan 2026) `[verified]`
- **Automation vs. learning:** she now builds both — AI teaching tools (Interview Coach, AI Interviewer, Business Fundamentals Coach) and discovery automation through Vistaly (interview snapshots and draft opportunity solution trees generated from three real interviews, alpha from Feb 2026). She keeps the caveat that some of the value of discovery is in doing it. (14 Jan, 18 Feb 2026) `[verified]`
- **Evals are a discovery habit:** she frames AI evals as a new discovery practice and publishes her own eval work on the Interview Coach and the AI tree generator. (3 Sep 2025; 2 and 16 Sep 2026) `[verified]`

**Whether the academy reflects it:**

- The on-demand Interview course does — the Coach is the product. `[verified]`
- Fundamentals' public pages mention no AI content. `[not found]`
- The AI-coached replacements for the other 12 habits have not shipped. `[verified]`

**What you would add yourself either way** `[judgment]`:

1. **Alembic's discipline on top of her two-step synthesis.** Her per-interview step is Alembic's atomize step; her cross-interview step is clustering. Add what she doesn't specify publicly: locators on every atom, distinct-person counts, contradictions reported as findings, and `[NOT IN SOURCE]` where the interviews are silent. Do your own snapshot first, then run the AI, then diff — her method and Alembic's agree here.
2. **Recipe A's prototyping speed for assumption tests.** Her assumption-testing material predates builds that take hours. At your speed, a prototype test on a real data room or a real stockpile is often cheaper than a survey — but it is still an assumption test, scoped to one assumption, with the success condition written first.
3. **Evals as part of DealReady discovery.** DealReady's product is AI output whose trust is the product. Her evals writing and the Just Now Possible episode on building AI for physicians at Healio (22 Jan 2026) are closer to your problem than anything in Fundamentals.

## 6. Discovery-practice ruling per product

### DealReady

**The finding that should shape everything:** the people you need to interview (investors doing diligence on Series A-and-later companies) overlap with the people Viewpoint is raising its seed round from. `[judgment, from context]` That is the dual-purpose case in Envoy's method, and it cuts in both directions: courtship bends questions, and a research conversation with a prospective investor is also a pitch whether you intend it or not.

**Rule:** keep two lists. Anyone in the active raise pipeline is not a research participant until the round closes; if a pipeline investor does get interviewed, that interview is logged as courtship-adjacent and its findings carry low confidence in the ledger. Miika (diligence advisor) is a referral source and one calibration interview, not a recurring participant — he's an insider.

**Cadence:** three conversations a week is the target; set a **floor of two** so a thin week doesn't break the habit. `[judgment]` Recruiting channels, in order: Kurt's and Miika's networks for investors *not* in the raise pipeline; associates and principals at funds that do Series A+ diligence (they do the data-room work and are easier to book than partners); design-partner framing once a prototype exists.

**Phase discipline:** your current direction for the product — investor skill profiles, a stage- and sector-branching question tree, a chat interface — is a set of **solutions**. None of it belongs in phase-one interviews, and none of it should seed the tree. The tree's opportunities come from what investors describe; your solutions attach under them later and are tested as assumptions.

**Outcome node** `[ASSUMPTION: product outcome, pre-launch]`: *Reduce the time from data-room access to a first risk judgment the investor trusts enough to act on.* Once live, this is measured as time to first trusted insight on a real deal room (Tally owns the definition).

**Interview guide shape (phase 1, ~30 min, story-based, no product reveal):**

1. Consent and framing, verbatim, including that nothing from their deals will be recorded in identifiable form.
2. *"Tell me about the last time you worked through a data room for a Series A or later deal."*
3. Set the scene: the deal's stage and sector, their role, the timeline, who else was involved.
4. Build the timeline: from getting access to the room, to the first thing they opened, to the investment-committee memo. Where did they slow down? What did they check twice? What did they not trust, and how did they decide whether to trust it?
5. Probe tools as behaviour, not opinion: what they used, including any AI, and exactly what they did when an output looked wrong.
6. Close with Envoy's two: *"What should I have asked you?"* and *"Who else should I talk to?"*
7. Within 24 hours: one interview snapshot per conversation (your own first), then an AI pass, then the diff — atoms with locators into the ledger.

**Assumption-test types that fit** `[judgment]`:

- **Trust (desirability):** "an investor will act on an AI claim when its source is one click away." Test with a Recipe-A prototype over a sample or public data room: record whether they click through to verify, and what they do after.
- **Access (viability and ethics):** "investors or founders will connect a real data room." Test by asking for it at the end of a phase-two session and recording the answer and the reason — a refusal on confidentiality grounds is a finding, not a failure.
- **Effort (desirability):** "an investor will spend time encoding their perspective." Test with a ten-minute version at the end of a phase-two session, and measure completion, not stated willingness.
- **Evals (feasibility and trust):** the model's risk claims against a small, labeled set of real diligence questions — Tally and the eval work own this, but its failure cases feed the tree.

**The standing disconfirmation priority** `[ASSUMPTION]`: *Investors will trust an AI-derived risk judgment enough to change what they do in diligence.* If that is false, the product is a search tool with a trust problem.

### Fybr

**The finding that should shape everything:** there are no self-serve users yet — design partners are one to two months out — so there are **no onboarding replays to review**. The textbook's "two conversations plus replays" cadence starts later than it assumes. `[judgment, from context]`

**Cadence now:** two conversations a week with people who own stockpile inventory numbers at pulp mills, sawmills, and pellet plants. **Cadence from the first design partner onward:** two conversations plus a replay review of each design partner's first measurement session within 48 hours. `[judgment]`

**Recruiting channel:** Fybr's full-service customers via Mike and the service team (the customer-facing-trigger strategy fits a services business exactly). Honest note on the frame: current full-service customers already outsource measurement, so they over-represent people who don't want to do it themselves. Include at least one non-customer operation per fortnight. `[judgment]`

**Outcome node** `[ASSUMPTION]`: *Reduce the time for a first-time operator to produce a stockpile measurement they're willing to use.* Measured later as self-serve time-to-first-measurement; trial-to-paid conversion is a downstream business outcome, not the tree's root.

**Interview guide shape (phase 1, ~30 min, story-based):**

1. *"Tell me about the last time you needed to know how much was in a pile."* (month-end count, audit, a purchasing decision, a dispute)
2. Scene: which site, which material, who needed the number and why, the deadline.
3. Timeline: who measured it and how; what the conditions were (weather, light, gloves, access); how they reconciled it to scale tickets or the books; who checked the number; what happened when it didn't match.
4. Trust: when was the last time they didn't believe a measurement, and what did they do?
5. Envoy's two closing questions; snapshot within 24 hours.

**Assumption-test types that fit** `[judgment]`:

- **Usability:** "a novice can capture a usable measurement in field conditions" — a field prototype test on a real pile, with the test run in the real light and gear.
- **Desirability and trust:** "an operator will use a self-captured number for reconciliation" — compare their self-measured number against their existing number and record what they'd report.
- **Viability:** "self-serve at price X is preferred to full service" — a pricing conversation in phase two, logged as stated preference (low confidence) until a design partner pays.

**The standing disconfirmation priority** `[ASSUMPTION]`: *Operators will trust a number they captured themselves enough to report it.* If not, self-serve becomes a lead-generation channel for the full-service business, which is a different product.
